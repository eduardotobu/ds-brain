## Matrices as linear transformations
A linear transformation is a way to set each point in the plane into another point in plane in a very structured way.

**A linear transformation is simply define as a change in set of coordinates.**

With linear transformations, the origin is always sent to the origin.

When you multiply a vector for a matrix, you are redefining the basis of that set of coordinates.

**A basis is the unit square of a set of coordinates.** **It covers all the linear space**

If you look through this example, vectors on the left conform the domain/source/input basis (the linear space before transformation) and the vectors in the right conform the codomain/target/output basis coordinates (the linear space after transformation) .
![[file-20260726163921863.jpg]]

As you can see, both vectors requires the same quantity of steps to reach the point in the linear space, in this case is 2 steps to the left and 3 steps up. So the matrix transforms the system of coordinates.

## Linear transformations as matrices

We obtained a linear transormation by a matrix, doing the same backwards is actually easy. Let's see a transformation and find the actual matrix. 

To find the actual matrix we only need the basis vectors. For the $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ space they will be (1,0) and (0,1). And also their equivalents after the linear transformation, for this case they will be (3,-1) and (2,3). So if you do reverse engineering in the math we saw previously (in linear transformations as matrices) it looks like this.
![[file-20260726171037472.jpg]]

So we can say that tecnically, a matrix can be seen as the new basis vectors for the new set of coordinates, first column is the new $i$, second column is the new $j$, third column is the new $k$ and so on.

### Understanding different effects on matrix multiplication.
To understand how a matrix changes a vector basis, there is one golden rule to keep in mind: the columns of a transformation matrix tell you exactly where your standard basis vectors land.If you start with the standard 2D grid defined by the horizontal step $\mathbf{e}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ and the vertical step $\mathbf{e}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$, multiplying by a matrix $M = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}$ maps those vectors to new coordinates:The first column $\begin{bmatrix} a \\ c \end{bmatrix}$ becomes the new x-basis vector ($\mathbf{e}_1'$).The second column $\begin{bmatrix} b \\ d \end{bmatrix}$ becomes the new y-basis vector ($\mathbf{e}_2'$).Every point in the space follows along linearly. Here is how that mechanics plays out across all six major geometric transformations.Interactive Transformation SimulatorBefore diving into the formulas below, use this interactive grid to apply preset transformations or manually tweak matrix entries $a, b, c,$ and $d$ to watch how the standard basis vectors and grid distort in real time:1. Shear Along the X-AxisA horizontal shear pushes the top of the coordinate grid to the right (or left) while leaving the x-axis itself completely pinned in place. The y-coordinates of points stay identical, but their x-coordinates shift proportionally to their height.The Transformation Matrix:$$M_{\text{shear x}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & k \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$(where $k$ is the shear factor)Basis Effect: The x-basis $\mathbf{e}_1$ stays at $\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$. The y-basis $\mathbf{e}_2$ tilts horizontally to land at $\begin{bmatrix} k \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.Example ($k = 2$): Transforming the point $(1, 3)$:$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 + 2(3) \\ 0 + 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$$2. Shear Along the Y-AxisA vertical shear slides vertical slices of the grid up or down. Points on the y-axis stay fixed, while points shift vertically depending on their horizontal distance from the y-axis.The Transformation Matrix:$$M_{\text{shear y}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ k & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$Basis Effect: The y-basis $\mathbf{e}_2$ stays at $\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. The x-basis $\mathbf{e}_1$ tilts vertically to land at $\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ k \end{bmatrix}$.Example ($k = -1.5$): Transforming the point $(2, 2)$:$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -1.5 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1.5(2) + 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$$3. Scaling (Dilation and Contraction)Scaling stretches or shrinks the coordinate grid along the axes. If a scaling factor is greater than 1, it is a dilation (expansion); if it is between 0 and 1, it is a contraction.The Transformation Matrix:$$M_{\text{scale}} = \begin{bmatrix} s_x & 0 \\ 0 & s_y \end{bmatrix}$$(where $s_x$ scales horizontally and $s_y$ scales vertically)Basis Effect: The basis vectors stay on their respective axes, but their lengths change. $\mathbf{e}_1$ becomes $\begin{bmatrix} s_x \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{e}_2$ becomes $\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ s_y \end{bmatrix}$.Example ($s_x = 3, s_y = 0.5$): Transforming the vector $\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$:$$\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$$4. Reflection (Around X, Y, or Both Axes)Reflection acts like a mirror, flipping the space across a line or across the origin. Notice that a single-axis reflection reverses the orientation of the grid (turning right-handed coordinates into left-handed ones), which gives the matrix a determinant of $-1$.Reflection Across X-Axis (Flips Y values):$$M_{\text{reflect x}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \quad \longrightarrow \quad \mathbf{e}_2 \text{ lands at } \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$$Reflection Across Y-Axis (Flips X values):$$M_{\text{reflect y}} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad \longrightarrow \quad \mathbf{e}_1 \text{ lands at } \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$Reflection Across Both Axes (Origin Reflection):$$M_{\text{reflect both}} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \quad \longrightarrow \quad \text{Both basis vectors point backward}$$5. Projection Onto One AxisProjection flattens the entire 2D space down onto a single 1D line, completely destroying any information along the perpendicular direction. Because an entire dimension is crushed, projection matrices always have a determinant of $0$ and cannot be inverted or reversed.Projection Onto the X-Axis (Flattens vertically):$$M_{\text{proj x}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$Basis Effect: $\mathbf{e}_1$ stays at $\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, but $\mathbf{e}_2$ is crushed to the origin $\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$.Example: $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$Projection Onto the Y-Axis (Flattens horizontally):$$M_{\text{proj y}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad \longrightarrow \quad \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 8 \end{bmatrix}$$6. Rotation (Clockwise and Counterclockwise)Rotation spins the entire coordinate grid around the origin by an angle $\theta$. Unlike shear or scaling, rotation is a rigid transformation—it preserves both the lengths of all vectors and the 90-degree angle between the basis vectors.Counterclockwise (CCW) Rotation by Angle $\theta$:$$M_{\text{CCW}} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$$Basis Effect: $\mathbf{e}_1$ rotates along the unit circle to $\begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta \\ \sin\theta \end{bmatrix}$, and $\mathbf{e}_2$ rotates 90° ahead of it to $\begin{bmatrix} -\sin\theta \\ \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$.Example (90° Counterclockwise, where $\cos 90^\circ = 0, \sin 90^\circ = 1$):$$M_{90^\circ \text{ CCW}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$$Clockwise (CW) Rotation by Angle $\theta$:To rotate clockwise, we replace $\theta$ with $-\theta$. Since $\cos(-\theta) = \cos\theta$ and $\sin(-\theta) = -\sin\theta$, the signs on the sine terms flip:$$M_{\text{CW}} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$$
